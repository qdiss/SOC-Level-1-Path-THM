## Overview

This is the first room of the Phishing Analysis module. It covers how
email works, what an email contains, and how to analyse a suspicious
message by examining its headers, body, and attachments. I practised
on sample emails in a lab virtual machine and learned to recognise the
common traits of phishing and related attacks.

## Objectives

- Learn the basics of email delivery
- Explore email header analysis
- Investigate and analyse email bodies
- Learn the different types of phishing
- Analyse emails to identify potential security threats

## Tools / Concepts Covered

- Structure of an email address (username, @ symbol, domain)
- Email protocols: SMTP for sending, POP3 and IMAP for retrieval
- The path of an email and the role of DNS in finding the recipient's mail server
- Email headers (From, To, Reply-To, Subject, Date, X-Originating-IP, Authentication-Results)
- Viewing raw message source in a mail client (Thunderbird)
- Attachment structure: Content-Type, Content-Disposition, base64 encoding
- CyberChef for decoding base64 and defanging URLs and IP addresses
- Types of malicious email: spam, phishing, spear phishing, whaling, smishing, vishing
- Business Email Compromise (BEC)

## Analysis Process

### Step 1 - Email Fundamentals

I started with how an email is delivered. The sender's client submits
the message to their mail server, that server uses DNS to find the
recipient's mail server, the message is delivered, and the recipient's
client then retrieves it. Retrieval differs by protocol: POP3 downloads
messages to a single device, while IMAP keeps them on the server and
syncs across devices. Knowing this path helps explain where header
information comes from.

### Step 2 - Analysing Headers

Using the sample emails in the lab VM, I opened the raw message source
and read the header fields beyond what the normal inbox shows. The
source reveals the real sender details, the originating IP address,
and authentication results generated by receiving mail servers. These
fields are where spoofing and unusual sending infrastructure are
often exposed.

### Step 3 - Analysing the Body and Attachments

I compared the rendered email with its underlying HTML to see links,
images, and embedded elements that may be hidden in the normal view,
including how mail clients block remote images by default. For an
attachment, the source shows the content type, filename, and encoding.
I reconstructed a base64-encoded attachment by decoding it with
CyberChef, which is a safe way to inspect a file without opening it in
the mail client.

### Step 4 - Recognising Phishing

I reviewed the categories of malicious email and the usual signs of
phishing: spoofed sender address, urgency, brand impersonation,
generic greetings, hidden or shortened links, and disguised
attachments. I also learned the safe-handling practice of defanging
URLs, domains, and IP addresses so they cannot be clicked by accident
while writing reports or sharing indicators.

### Step 5 - Applying It to a Sample

Finally, I analysed a sample phishing email: identifying the brand
being impersonated, the sender, the originating IP (defanged), and the
mail server that produced the authentication results. The room closed
with a short introduction to Business Email Compromise, where a
legitimate but compromised account is used to commit fraud.

## Outcome / Classification

Completed the email structure, header, attachment, and phishing
identification questions, and classified the final sample email as
phishing based on the evidence in its headers and content.

## Key Takeaways

The raw message source is the most reliable evidence in an email
investigation, because the inbox view shows only what the sender wants
the user to see. Defanging and analysing attachments in a controlled
environment are habits I should apply from the start, since one
accidental click can defeat the purpose of the analysis. This room
also connects to my earlier SOC Simulator phishing scenario, where I
had to judge phishing alerts quickly and write clear reports.
